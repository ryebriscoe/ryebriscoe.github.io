A research paper on the use of unmanned aerial vehicles in disaster response and humanitarian aid, written in APA format and drawing on eight peer reviewed and institutional sources.

The paper opens on the problem that medical rescue teams can take hours or days to reach survivors, while the first 72 hours after a disaster are the ones that matter most. It traces drone technology from Tesla's remote controlled boat in 1898 through military development and into humanitarian use after the 2010 Haiti and 2015 Nepal earthquakes. It then examines what the technology actually does in the field, covering aerial imaging and damage assessment, autonomous navigation, supply delivery, and the tradeoffs between fixed wing, multi rotor, and fully autonomous platforms.

The second half argues the harder side. Aerial surveillance during an emergency captures people who never consented to being recorded, and there is little regulation governing how that data is stored or used. Uncoordinated drone traffic over a disaster zone can obstruct the rescue operations it is meant to support. Mechanical failure and signal loss put both victims and responders at risk. The paper closes on unequal access, since wealthier countries and well funded organizations get the benefit of this technology while low income and isolated communities are left without it, which undercuts the humanitarian premise the whole field rests on.
