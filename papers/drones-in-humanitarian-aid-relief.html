Drones in Humanitarian Aid Relief

Ariya R. Briscoe · April 6, 2025

When natural disasters occur, every second counts, but National Medical Rescue Teams (NMRT) and Disaster Medical Assistance Teams (DMAT) can take hours or even days to reach survivors (Wanner & Loyd, 2022). The first 72 hours are critical, and delays in team responses can mean the difference between life and death. With further technological advancements, drones are key in helping lay a new foundation for these rescue efforts. This essay will explore how Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), or drones, have evolved from military tools into essential emergency response and humanitarian aid tools. This paper examines the technical capabilities, historical development, and challenges associated with using drones in disaster relief. This paper analyzes both their benefits and concerns that display how UAVs are reshaping the future of disaster management and response.

Historical Development of Drone Technology

Drones, or Unmanned Aerial Vehicles, are modern technological devices capable of performing functions that can help with disaster response, like infrastructure monitoring, heat signature readings, and precise delivery.

Military Origins and Early Developments

The origins of drone technology date back to the late 19th century when Nikola Tesla demonstrated a remote-controlled boat in 1898, sparking interest in autonomous aircraft (Piesing, 2024). Early drone prototypes were developed for military use during World War I, but they faced issues with their reliability. Advancements during World War II helped refine radio-controlled flight, also known as remote-controlled, which set the foundation for modern UAVs. Throughout the Cold War, drones became progressively advanced, especially in the field of surveillance operations during the Vietnam War (Piesing, 2024). As drone technology improved, its practical uses expanded beyond military use only.

Expansion into Humanitarian and Disaster Response

By the 21st century, drones were becoming more widespread. They were used in other areas outside the military, particularly in disaster response, where events like the Haiti earthquake in 2010 and the Nepal earthquake in 2015 allowed the drones' benefits to be highlighted for their potential for search-and-rescue and aerial damage assessments.

Technical Capabilities of Drones in Humanitarian Aid

Aerial Imaging and Damage Assessment

Drones possess key functions that significantly increase response time and efforts to emergencies and humanitarian needs. These drones can utilize advanced aerial imaging, navigation without human interaction, and the capability to transport and deliver necessary materials. Aerial imaging technology allows drones to quickly provide high-resolution visual data, which makes it easier and more accessible to assess the scale of areas affected by disasters (Gholami, 2024). Drones have the ability to identify structural damage in buildings and locate lost individuals, even in regions that are very dangerous for people.

Autonomous Navigation and Operation

Autonomous navigation capabilities allow drones to fly independently without human control. This reduces the time needed for remote operations, which can be very valuable in scenarios where conditions can harm humans. It also enables drones to be deployed immediately without waiting for human interaction.

Delivery and Supply Capabilities

Delivery capabilities further allow drones to support human relief in the form of transporting essential supplies ranging from medical equipment and pharmaceuticals to food and water in areas unreachable by traditional ways of travel.

Types of UAVs Used in Disasters

Furthermore, Gholami identifies the common drones used for disaster scenarios: fixed-wing, multi-rotor, and fully autonomous drones. Fixed-wing drones are often deployed for extensive area coverage due to their endurance and range capabilities, making them ideal for surveying missions traveling across long ranges and post-disaster. In contrast, multi-rotor drones, which are known for their agility, vertical takeoff and landing capabilities, and stability, make them perfect for photography, and they are frequently deployed for accurate targeting, for instance, victim identification and targeted supply drops. Autonomous drones, a mixture of previous drones with the addition of advanced artificial intelligence, significantly enhance efficiency and responsiveness during emergency operations by independently adapting to hazardous conditions. The integration of these drone technologies emphasizes their role in humanitarian disaster response, directly contributing to increased efficiency.

Challenges of Traditional Disaster Response

Accessibility and Delays in Victim Location

Traditional disaster response efforts consist of search and rescue and medical teams. However, these conventional strategies can bring up many challenges, resulting in delays. These delays in crucial rescue operations can put human responders and those caught in the disaster even more at risk. According to researchers at PennState, "Search-and-rescue teams often face life-threatening delays due to collapsed infrastructure, impassable terrain, and hazardous conditions, significantly reducing survival rates in disaster-stricken areas" (Glantz et al., 2020). One major issue is locating victims in disaster zones, mainly where the land is damaged; collapsed and unstable structures or flooded areas make access difficult on foot. Even in these harsh conditions, search-and-rescue teams must traverse hazardous environments, often leading to delays that can be life-threatening.

Risk to Human Responders

Moreover, human responders face high levels of risk themselves, especially in dangerous environments such as earthquake aftermaths or zones affected by wildfires. Traditional response teams' limited access to remote or isolated areas makes it more difficult to give support, assess damage, and deliver aid to those in urgent need.

Benefits and Advantages of Drone Integration

Enhanced Search and Rescue Efficiency

Drone technology provides solutions to these challenges accompanied by traditional methods by offering quick aerial mapping, real-time danger assessments, and efficient skills at locating victims. Drones tasked with disaster response are equipped with infrared and high-resolution cameras with the ability to perform thermal imaging. Studies show LiDAR UAVs can quickly survey disaster zones by shooting millions of laser pulses at the speed of light, which bounce off the surfaces to measure distance, identify structural damage with lasers that create 3-dimensional data points, and detect heat signatures of trapped individuals in areas blocked to ground teams (NOAA, 2024). This reduces search times and allows rescue teams to focus efforts where needed.

Speed and Accessibility to Isolated Areas

Additionally, drones are key in delivering essential supplies, such as medical kits, food, and clean water, directly to victims in areas cut off from ground teams and contact with help. Their ability to get past blocked roads or dangerous areas by flight significantly improves how fast response teams can locate and assist victims. According to a systematic review, drones reached victims up to 93% faster and covered nearly twice the surface area compared to ground teams (Claesson, 2017).

Improved Coordination and Communication

Furthermore, drones are equipped with live-streaming, giving real-time views and enhancing coordination between emergency responders. Live view of drone footage assists with decision-making and resource allocation during disaster response. As UAV technology progresses, its use in disaster management continues to be effective in providing quick, cost-effective, and scalable solutions. The addition of UAVs into emergency response frameworks allows response teams to maximize disaster relief efforts, prevent rescuers from potentially dangerous surroundings, and increase overall successful rescues in areas impacted by disasters.

Ethical Concerns and Risks

Privacy and Surveillance Issues

Despite the benefits, the increasing number of UAVs used in disaster relief also raises concerns. One significant issue that comes up is the potential violation of privacy, as aerial surveillance happens during emergencies; recordings and live streams may capture personal data or infringe upon the rights of affected populations. According to Khan (2025), UAVs can threaten individual privacy. They can record, store, and send data that could be used to accuse people, which is why many people question unauthorized surveillance. With UAVs being a new and still testable technology, regulations are constantly being altered, and the grey area of what they should be capable of doing is still undetermined.

Operational Challenges and Airspace Coordination

Additionally, drones in disaster zones can create risks for rescue operations, mainly with multiple agencies or private parties involved, without coordinating plans. This issue can slow emergency response teams and create challenges for open airspace.

Regulatory and Ethical Accountability

Moreover, depending on drone technology raises concerns, as mechanical problems, signal interruptions, or severe weather can lead to crashes or glitches that could harm both victims and rescue teams. Drones have changed how teams handle disasters, yet efficient regulations, better coordination, and technological improvements are essential for using drones to their advantage and limiting potential risks.

While drones provide many advantages in disaster response, their growing presence in communities also introduces ethical concerns that go beyond technological issues. One of the most serious issues is the lack of clear regulations regarding how UAVs collect and use data during emergencies. In emergencies, drones are dispatched, often forgoing the process of following regulations (Bradley & Chiou, 2024). Unlike typical surveillance tools used in public places following guidelines, drones can easily gather personal and sensitive information without the knowledge or consent of those affected because of their ability to fly over restricted and private areas not open to the public. This raises questions about privacy rights being undermined, especially in vulnerable communities. Beyond privacy, serious questions regarding responsibility and who takes accountability for one's actions are in question. Who is to blame when a drone causes harm? Are developers, operators, or emergency agencies deploying them? Establishing who is legally responsible is crucial in environments where human lives are at risk.

Unequal Access and Ethical Implications

There is growing concern over the unequal access to drone technology. Wealthier countries and heavily funded organizations benefit from drones, while low-income countries or isolated communities are left behind, unable to aid in similar disaster responses. This inequality goes against fair and equal humanitarian relief (Bradley & Chiou, 2024). Without active engagement with policies and an understanding of ethical situations, drones may inadvertently promote one-sidedness in disaster management.

Conclusion

Unmanned Aerial Vehicles have emerged as life-changing tools in modern disaster response and humanitarian aid. From their beginnings as military technologies to their current use in health emergencies, UAVs are necessary to reduce response times, improve victim locating, and deliver life-saving support when traditional response teams' access is delayed or impossible. Their advanced imaging systems, autonomous navigation, and adaptability make them essential in traversing disaster zones more quickly, efficiently, and safely than traditional methods. However, with drone advancements come new ethical and operational challenges. Privacy concerns, regulating use, and unequal access present notable issues that policies must address. As drone technology continues to evolve, emergency response systems must use UAVs responsibly and objectively. This will make certain that their full potential for life-saving is fully seen and used without compromising safety, ethics, or fairness, ultimately reshaping the future of humanitarian relief for the better.
